Work Prompt — SONAR Measurement: Complete Reconstruction and Evidence Analysis
1. Mission and Scope
You are working on SONAR (System for Optical Normalization and Analysis of RNA Editing), a four-layer quantitative measurement framework developed for the SUSTech-China iGEM 2026 project.
Your task is to reconstruct the SONAR analysis pipeline entirely from scratch, using only the original experimental data from S7 and S10, and to produce a comprehensive, scientifically rigorous evidence package for the subsequent development of our iGEM Wiki Measurement page.
This is a fresh implementation, not an update to any previous SONAR build.
Several earlier versions of SONAR have already been developed or attempted. However, their implementations, intermediate results, parameter choices, and conclusions must not be inherited.
Start again from the original S7 and S10 experimental data.
The accompanying SONAR_Work_EN_v3.1.md is provided as a reference for the scientific architecture, mathematical definitions, and intended measurement objectives.
It is not an implementation that must be continued, nor does it establish that any previous analysis was correct.
Your responsibilities are to:
- Reconstruct the data-processing and quantitative-analysis pipeline.
- Verify the scientific and statistical validity of its key components.
- Perform all analyses that can be supported by the available S7/S10 data.
- Evaluate the feasibility and limitations of downstream calibration.
- Generate reproducible analytical results and supporting visualizations.
- Prepare a structured evidence package for subsequent Wiki writing and figure production.
Do not write the final Wiki page or produce publication-ready Wiki figures in this stage.
Those tasks will be completed separately after the scientific results have been reviewed.

5. Phase III — Layer 1: Flow Quantification
Layer 1 is the primary quantitative focus of this reconstruction.
5.1 Main estimator
Preserve the scientific definition of the Reporter Activity Score:
\[
\boxed{
RAS_{\mathcal P}
=
\frac{\sum_{i\in\mathcal P}g_i}
{\sum_{i\in\mathcal P}r_i}
}
\]
where:
\[
g_i=G_i-\widehat b_{G,i}
\]
\[
r_i=R_i-\widehat b_{R,i}
\]
The corrected red and green values must come from exactly the same selected events.
RAS is the ratio of population-level fluorescence sums, not the arithmetic mean of individual cell ratios.
Do not:
- Gate on EGFP positivity when computing the primary RAS.
- Remove EGFP-negative cells solely because they are green-negative.
- Rectify negative background-corrected green values to zero.
- Introduce an arbitrary denominator epsilon.
- Compute the ratio using display-transformed values.
- Silently truncate high-intensity events.
Establish an empirically justified requirement for adequate mCherry reference signal and denominator precision.
If a sample cannot support a reliable RAS estimate, return an explicit unavailable status rather than an artificial numerical result.
5.2 Analysis populations
Support two clearly distinguished population modes.
Reporter population:
\[
\mathcal P_R=\mathcal V\cap R_q^+
\]
Reporter–editor co-expression population:
\[
\mathcal P_{RB}=\mathcal V\cap R_q^+\cap B^+
\]
The first measures the normalized reporter response within the quantitatively usable reporter-expressing population.
The second measures the response conditional on detectable reporter and editor-linked BFP expression.
Compute both modes when the experimental construct and data support them.
Do not force samples without editor-linked BFP into the co-expression mode.
Report co-expression fractions and relevant population counts separately.
5.3 Quantitative RAS analysis
Calculate the primary RAS independently for each valid experimental well.
Provide:
- Raw green fluorescence summaries.
- Corrected green fluorescence summaries.
- Corrected red fluorescence summaries.
- RAS.
- EGFP-positive fraction as an auxiliary metric.
- Reporter-positive fraction.
- Reporter–editor co-expression fraction.
- Selected event counts.
- Quantification and QC status.
- Appropriate uncertainty estimates.
Maintain all results at the correct independent experimental unit.
Do not pool events from independent wells and interpret the resulting event count as biological replication.
5.4 Evaluate mCherry normalization
A central scientific question is:
Does continuous mCherry normalization improve the interpretability and repeatability of reporter measurements?
Use appropriate S7/S10 comparisons to evaluate this question.
Where experimental conditions and independent replicates permit, compare:
- Raw EGFP fluorescence.
- Background-corrected EGFP fluorescence.
- RAS.
Evaluate well-to-well variability using clearly defined statistics.
If coefficient of variation is used, state exactly how it was calculated and which independent wells contribute.
Do not treat different biological conditions as technical replicates.
Importantly, a reduction in variability is not automatically evidence of biological accuracy.
Assess whether RAS behaves consistently across the relevant reporter-expression range.
Where possible, distinguish variation in common expression scale from genuine effects of changing reporter abundance on the biological response.
5.5 Evaluate BFP as an additional measurement dimension
Determine what information is gained by detecting editor-linked BFP.
Using eligible S10 samples, investigate:
- BFP-positive fractions.
- Reporter–editor co-expression fractions.
- BFP intensity distributions.
- Differences between reporter-population and co-expression RAS.
- Associations with experimental editor DNA loading.
Where there is sufficient common signal support, explore response profiles within matched BFP-intensity ranges.
Do not interpret BFP fluorescence as an exact measure of active ORCA molecular abundance.
Do not automatically interpret an increasing BFP-positive fraction as increasing RNA-editing efficiency.
Separate observable co-expression behavior from biological editing activity.
5.6 Robustness and uncertainty
Perform feasible robustness analyses using the actual data.
Prioritize:
Event-count sensitivity
Investigate how RAS varies with repeated subsampling of selected events.
Background sensitivity
Evaluate the effect of reasonable, justified changes in background estimation.
Gate sensitivity
Assess the influence of reasonable changes in population-selection boundaries.
High-expression influence
Check whether a small number of highly expressing cells disproportionately determine the population RAS.
Well-level repeatability
Evaluate variation between independent wells under matched experimental conditions.
Population-mode sensitivity
Determine how the reported response changes between reporter-population and co-expression modes.
Clearly distinguish uncertainty due to event sampling, controls, and independent experimental replication.

10. Scientific Integrity Requirements
Throughout this project, follow these rules.
No inherited results.
All experimental statistics must be derived anew from authorized S7/S10 data.
No outcome-driven optimization.
Do not select gates, background estimators, or scoring formulas because they produce desirable biological rankings.
No unsupported calibration.
Do not report RES or PER as validated measurements without appropriate reference and independent validation evidence.
No false replication.
Do not use event counts as biological sample sizes.
No invented data.
Never synthesize experimental observations, replicate wells, molecular editing measurements, or successful calibration curves.
No unsupported universality.
Measurement conclusions apply only to experimental conditions supported by the actual evidence.
No conflation of metrics.
Keep fluorescence intensity, RAS, RES, PER, reference DNA fraction, and independently measured RNA-editing fraction conceptually distinct.
No silent correction of inconsistencies.
When experimental records disagree, document and resolve the discrepancy from primary evidence wherever possible.
No unnecessary complexity.
Prefer scientifically interpretable, well-supported methods over more sophisticated algorithms that cannot be validated with the available data.
No automatic rejection of inconvenient results.
Unexpected, weak, or nonmonotonic findings must be investigated rather than removed to improve the narrative.
